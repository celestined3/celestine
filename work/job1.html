Modular Deployable Large Space Tensegrity Structures - SoE Internship

Overview

This internship provided me a unique opportunity to contribute to the startup of a new project, with the aim of securing university funding by the end of the summer. Initially, I 3D modelled and printed small rods and caps in order to make 3-rod tensegrity structures. Experimenting with rods and strings taught me the importance of iterations in design, with my final Solidworks design proving to be the most practical and efficient for manufacture. This provided a simple foundation from which my colleagues could create higher order tensegrities (4,9 and 10-rod models). The use of Solidworks allowed me to alter material properties of the components so that I could test them before printing, which highlighted the realistic applications of my design decisions.

Once I became confident in my understanding of tensegrity structures, I decided to apply that to a practical example. Using Python scripting in Abaqus, I created a tensegrity structure network in the shape of NASA’s proposed LCRT (Lunar Crater Radio Telescope). This not only pushed me out of my comfort zone but it greatly increased my proficiency in both Python and Abaqus. The aspect of this project that I found the most exciting was its open nature because it encouraged creativity and independent problem solving – I scripted many different models in Python and ran Finite Element Analysis tests on them in Abaqus in order to demonstrate their behaviour in an environment that reflects that of the moon.

The tools that I used throughout this internship enabled me to carry out an in-depth analysis on the effects of changing certain aspects of the design of the lunar crater tensegrity network, such as its material properties, length of rods, number of strings, environment parameters and deployment mechanisms. I quickly realised that engineering is a constant iteration loop, where all characteristics are dynamic and dependent on each other. As a space enthusiast, I really enjoyed doing research about the harsh space environment and at the end of the summer, it felt very rewarding when my team received funding from the ESA for the next 15 years for this project! I am looking forward to the future of the next phase of this project – deploying the tensegrity network.
